A Medical Revolution for HIV in Kenya

How a twice-yearly injectable drug, USAID funding cuts, and Kenya's most vulnerable communities converged to reshape the future of HIV prevention in sub-Saharan Africa.

01 · Background

How Did We Get Here?

Kenya's HIV epidemic began in 1984, but meaningful political action lagged for over a decade. Financial barriers, inadequate sanitation infrastructure, and deeply entrenched stigma allowed the virus to spread unchecked. By the early 2000s, HIV prevalence had become a national crisis.

The landscape changed dramatically in 2003 when the United States launched PEPFAR under President George W. Bush. Over the next two decades, consistent U.S. investment helped drive Kenya's HIV prevalence from 7.4% down to 3.2%. It was one of global health's great success stories — until 2025.

The sudden withdrawal of USAID and PEPFAR funding triggered an alarming reversal. New infection rates climbed 19% in a single year. AIDS-related deaths rose. And the communities left most exposed were those already most marginalized — particularly young women and girls, who now account for 66% of new infections.

02 · Timeline

The Arc of Kenya's HIV Crisis

1984

First Recorded Case

Kenya identifies its first HIV case. Initial responses are slow, characterized by political denial and social stigma that prevent effective early intervention.

2003

PEPFAR Launches

The U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief begins funding Kenya's HIV response, injecting resources that would transform the country's epidemic trajectory over the following two decades.

2023

Two Decades of Progress

U.S. investment has driven Kenya's HIV rate from 7.4% to 3.2%. The Kenya AIDS Progress Report records 16,752 new cases — still high, but representing real progress from prior peaks.

2024–25

Funding Cuts & Resurgence

USAID and PEPFAR cuts take effect. New HIV cases spike 19% to 19,991. AIDS-related deaths rise 3%. The Georgetown Center for Global Health Policy warns the global HIV response faces an unprecedented threat.

2025–26

Lenacapavir Approved & Deployed

The WHO approves Lenacapavir in July 2025 following landmark clinical trials. Kenya rolls out the twice-yearly injectable in March 2026, beginning in 15 high-burden counties.

"The global HIV response is under threat like never before, with these drastic funding cuts undermining the gains of the past 25 years."

Sharonann Lynch, Co-Director, Georgetown University Center for Global Health Policy and Politics, 2025

03 · Medical Prevention

PrEP: Kenya's Three Pathways

Medical prevention of HIV — pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP — is available in Kenya through three primary methods. Each has distinct advantages and real-world limitations. Understanding adherence barriers is essential to understanding why Lenacapavir matters.

01

Daily Oral Pills

The most established form of PrEP, oral pills require consistent daily use for full protection. Adherence rates are declining sharply — from 82% at 8 weeks to just 62% at 52 weeks — making real-world effectiveness far lower than clinical trial results.

High Adherence Burden
02

Intravaginal Ring

A monthly hormone-releasing ring designed specifically for cisgender women. While it removes the daily pill burden, it still requires monthly replacement and consistent correct use, creating a moderate behavior burden that limits effectiveness in real-world settings.

Moderate Adherence Burden
03

Long-Acting Injectables

Long-acting injectable PrEP dramatically reduces the behavior burden by requiring only a few administrations per year. Lenacapavir — administered just twice yearly — is the newest and most potent entry in this category, with near-perfect protection in trials.

Low Adherence Burden
04 · The Drug

Lenacapavir: A Twice-Yearly Breakthrough

Lenacapavir is a capsid inhibitor — a novel class of HIV drug that works by blocking the protein shell (capsid) protecting the virus's genetic material. Unlike daily pills, a single injection provides six months of protection, fundamentally changing the compliance equation.

Its approval was driven by two landmark clinical trials — the PURPOSE trials — conducted across Southern Africa and beyond. The results were extraordinary enough to shift the standard of care conversation worldwide.

Kenya began its national rollout in March 2026, starting with 15 high-burden counties. Two additional phases will progressively expand coverage, ensuring supply chain readiness and sustainable scale-up across all 47 counties.

PURPOSE 1 Trial · Bekker et al., NEJM 2024

5,338 cisgender women across South Africa and Uganda received either Lenacapavir or daily F/TAF. Among those on Lenacapavir, zero participants contracted HIV over the study period.

100% Protection Rate among Lenacapavir participants

PURPOSE 2 Trial · Kelley et al., NEJM 2025

3,265 men and gender-diverse individuals enrolled across multiple countries. Only 2 participants taking Lenacapavir contracted HIV — a rate significantly lower than the general population.

2 Infections out of 3,265 Lenacapavir participants

Kenya Rollout · Ministry of Health, 2026

Phase 1 targets 15 high-burden counties. Plans include a three-phase expansion ensuring service preparedness, reliable commodity supply, and sustainable national coverage.

06 · Financial Access

Breaking the Cost Barrier

A drug that works is only transformative if people can access it. The original market price of Lenacapavir posed a serious barrier. Kenya's path forward depends on two parallel strategies: localized manufacturing and telehealth-enabled delivery.

South Africa's response to the funding cuts offers a powerful model: by building domestic manufacturing infrastructure, it reduced costs dramatically and is now pioneering the continent's first African-scientist-led HIV vaccine trial.

Global market price (pre-localization) ~$42,000 / year
Kenya projected cost (with local supply) ~$6.60 / year

Source: Ministry of Health Kenya, 2026

🏭

Local Manufacturing Pipelines

Following South Africa's lead, Kenya is building domestic supply chains that reduce dependence on global pharmaceutical markets and international aid — cutting costs by over 99%.

📱

Telehealth Drug Delivery

The EPrEP Kenya pilot (Kiptinness et al., 2025) demonstrated that online HIV prevention delivery — virtual consultations, home test kits, home delivery — can bring visit costs down to just a few dollars while protecting patient privacy.

🔬

African-Led Research

South Africa is running the first HIV vaccine trial led entirely by African scientists — a model Kenya should adopt and expand.

🏥

Phased National Rollout

Kenya's three-phase Lenacapavir deployment begins in 15 high-burden counties, ensuring service readiness and supply reliability before progressive national scale-up.

07 · Stakeholders

Who Shapes This Story?

Understanding HIV prevention requires understanding the actors whose decisions, interests, and vulnerabilities define what happens on the ground.

Kenyan Government

Ministry of Health & NASCOP

Responsible for national HIV policy, drug procurement, and the Lenacapavir rollout. With PEPFAR funding gone, the government must now lead domestic financing and expand local healthcare infrastructure — a challenge and an opportunity to build lasting self-sufficiency.

Pharmaceutical Industry

Gilead Sciences & Generic Manufacturers

Gilead Sciences developed Lenacapavir. Critically, they have issued voluntary licenses to generic manufacturers in low- and middle-income countries, enabling the dramatic cost reduction to ~$6.60/year that makes the Kenya rollout financially viable.

Most Vulnerable Population

Young Women & Girls

Women account for 66% of Kenya's new HIV infections — a disproportion driven not by biology but by systemic gender inequality, economic dependence, and constrained healthcare access. Any HIV strategy that doesn't center this group will fail.

International Community

WHO, Researchers & Global Advocates

The WHO's July 2025 Lenacapavir approval, the PURPOSE trial researchers, and advocates like Georgetown's Sharonann Lynch provide the evidence base and political pressure necessary to keep HIV prevention on the global agenda despite funding retreats.

08 · Reflection

What I Learned — and Why It Matters

I became interested in the AIDS epidemic after learning about the 2025 USAID funding cuts and the resulting concerns about a resurgence in HIV transmission. As someone who hopes to pursue a career in medical research while remaining deeply committed to social justice, I was especially struck by the gender disparities surrounding HIV infection rates. In the United States, HIV is often discussed as a disease that disproportionately affects men. However, while researching Kenya, I discovered that women and girls are among the most vulnerable populations, shaped by systemic inequalities that extend far beyond biology alone.

This research taught me the importance of approaching research from an interdisciplinary perspective. In order to fully understand HIV transmission in Kenya, I had to examine not only medicine and epidemiology, but also poverty, gender inequality, healthcare infrastructure, political instability, international aid, and pharmaceutical access. Reading broadly allowed me to better situate my research question within a larger global context and recognize how interconnected these issues truly are.

In addition, this research allowed me to deepen my scientific understanding of HIV itself, including how the virus attacks the immune system, how antiretroviral therapies function, and how emerging prevention strategies such as PrEP and Lenacapavir are changing the future of HIV prevention and treatment. Learning about these medical and scientific advances made me even more interested in infectious disease research and the ways biomedical innovation can improve public health outcomes on a global scale. More than anything, this experience reinforced my belief that scientific progress cannot be separated from questions of equity, accessibility, and human rights.
