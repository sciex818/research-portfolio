Research Context

HIV affects 40.8 million people worldwide, with 1.3 million of those cases located in Kenya. As worldwide case reports have decreased, provinces in Kenya are experiencing an alarming uptick in HIV transmission — linked to lower gender equality, higher stigmatization, and economic deprivation.

The 2025 cuts to USAID and PEPFAR have had a detrimental effect on Kenya's HIV prevention measures. Yet this crisis has also spurred local government engagement and medical innovation. A new long-lasting injectable drug — Lenacapavir — was approved by the WHO in 2025 and presents a revolutionary step forward.

"The global HIV response is under threat like never before, with these drastic funding cuts undermining the gains of the past 25 years."
— Sharonann Lynch, Georgetown University Center for Global Health Policy, 2025

02 · Background

The Arc of Kenya's
HIV Crisis

PERIOD: 1984–2026
TYPE: EPIDEMIOLOGICAL HISTORY
Historical Timeline
1984

First Recorded Case

Kenya identifies its first HIV case. Initial political responses are slow, characterized by denial through the late 1990s. Financial, sanitation, and healthcare barriers allow the epidemic to spread.

2003

PEPFAR Launches

Under President George W. Bush, the U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief begins sustained investment in Kenya's HIV response — a turning point in the country's epidemic trajectory.

2003–2023

Two Decades of Progress

U.S. investment drives Kenya's HIV prevalence from 7.4% down to 3.2% over twenty years — a landmark achievement in global public health. New annual cases fall to 16,752 by 2023.

2024–2025

Funding Cuts & Resurgence

USAID and PEPFAR cuts take effect. New HIV cases spike 19% to 19,991 in a single year. AIDS-related deaths rise 3% to 21,007. Women represent 66% of new infections.

Critical Inflection Point
2025–2026

Lenacapavir Approved & Deployed

WHO approves Lenacapavir in July 2025 following PURPOSE trial results. Kenya begins its national rollout in March 2026, targeting 15 high-burden counties in Phase 1.

03 · Medical Prevention

PrEP: Kenya's Three
Prevention Pathways

MODALITY: PRE-EXPOSURE PROPHYLAXIS
CONTEXT: KENYA NATIONAL PROGRAM

Medical prevention of HIV — pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) — is available in Kenya through three primary methods. Each has distinct clinical advantages and real-world adherence limitations. Understanding these gaps is essential to understanding why Lenacapavir represents such a critical advance.

Method 01

Daily Oral Pills

The most established form of PrEP. Requires consistent daily use for full protection. Real-world adherence is declining sharply — from 82% at 8 weeks to just 62% at 52 weeks — making clinical efficacy difficult to achieve in practice.

Intravaginal Ring

A monthly hormone-releasing ring designed specifically for cisgender women. Removes the daily pill burden but still requires monthly replacement and consistent correct use — a moderate behavior burden limiting effectiveness.

Method 03

Long-Acting Injectables

Dramatically reduces the behavior burden by requiring only a few administrations per year. Lenacapavir — administered just twice yearly — is the newest and most potent entry in this category, with near-perfect protection in clinical trials.

Core Insight

The adherence issues associated with daily oral pills and intravaginal rings are largely bypassed by the low-maintenance nature of long-acting injectables. A patient who only needs to visit a clinic twice a year — rather than taking a pill every single day — is far more likely to maintain full protection over time.

This is why Lenacapavir's six-month dosing interval is not merely a convenience — it is a clinical breakthrough in real-world effectiveness.

04 · The Drug

Lenacapavir:
A Twice-Yearly Breakthrough

CLASS: CAPSID INHIBITOR
DOSING: EVERY 6 MONTHS
WHO APPROVAL: JULY 2025

Lenacapavir is a capsid inhibitor — a novel class of HIV drug that works by blocking the protein shell (capsid) protecting the virus's genetic material. A single injection provides six months of protection, fundamentally transforming the adherence equation for HIV prevention.

Its approval was driven by two landmark clinical trials — the PURPOSE trials — conducted across Southern Africa and beyond. The results were extraordinary: near-total elimination of new HIV infections among participants receiving the drug.

5,338 cisgender women received either Lenacapavir or daily F/TAF. Among those on Lenacapavir, zero participants contracted HIV over the study period.

Solution Pathways
🏭

Local Manufacturing Pipelines

Following South Africa's lead, Kenya is building domestic supply chains that reduce dependence on global pharmaceutical markets — cutting costs from $42,000 to just $6.60 per patient annually.

📱

Telehealth Drug Delivery

The EPrEP Kenya pilot demonstrated that online HIV prevention — virtual consultations, home test kits, home medication delivery — brings visit costs down to a few dollars while protecting patient privacy in high-stigma communities.

🔬

African-Led Research

South Africa is running the first HIV vaccine trial led entirely by African scientists — bringing drug and vaccine development to the continent rather than importing it. Kenya should follow this model.

🏥

Phased National Rollout

Kenya's three-phase Lenacapavir deployment begins in 15 high-burden counties, ensuring service readiness and supply reliability before progressive national scale-up across all 47 counties.

"Without deliberate policy and financing interventions, new prevention technologies such as Lenacapavir risk following slow adoption patterns of previous innovations, weakening a needed transformation of the HIV response."
— Lynch et al., The Lancet HIV, October 2025
06 · Stakeholders

Who Shapes
This Story?

ANALYSIS: MULTI-ACTOR
FRAMEWORK: INTERESTS + ROLES

Understanding HIV prevention in Kenya requires understanding the actors whose decisions, interests, and vulnerabilities define what actually happens on the ground. Each stakeholder brings a distinct perspective — and a distinct set of stakes.

Kenyan Government

Ministry of Health & NASCOP

Responsible for national HIV policy, drug procurement, and the Lenacapavir rollout. With PEPFAR funding withdrawn, the government must now lead domestic financing and expand local healthcare infrastructure — a challenge and an opportunity to build lasting self-sufficiency.

Policy + Procurement
Pharmaceutical Industry

Gilead Sciences & Generic Manufacturers

Gilead Sciences developed Lenacapavir. Critically, they have issued voluntary licenses to generic manufacturers in low- and middle-income countries — enabling the dramatic cost reduction from $42,000 to $6.60/year that makes the Kenya rollout financially viable.

Innovation + Licensing
Most Vulnerable Population

Young Women & Girls

Women account for 66% of Kenya's new HIV infections — a disproportion driven not by biology but by systemic gender inequality, economic dependence, and constrained healthcare access. Any HIV strategy that fails to center this group will fail overall.

Equity + Access
International Community

WHO, Researchers & Global Advocates

The WHO's 2025 Lenacapavir approval, the PURPOSE trial researchers, and advocates like Georgetown's Sharonann Lynch provide the evidence base and political pressure necessary to keep HIV prevention on the global agenda despite funding retreats.

07 · Reflection

What I Learned —
and Why It Matters

TYPE: PERSONAL REFLECTION
CONTEXT: IWA RESEARCH PAPER

I became interested in this topic after learning about the 2025 USAID funding cuts and their public health consequences. As someone who hopes to pursue a career in medical research with a deep commitment to social justice, I was struck by the gender dimensions of HIV in Kenya.

In the United States, HIV is often discussed as disproportionately affecting men. Yet in Kenya, women and girls are the most exposed population — shaped by systemic inequalities that extend far beyond biology. This research taught me that medicine cannot be separated from economics, politics, and human rights.

This project also deepened my scientific understanding of how HIV attacks the immune system, how antiretroviral therapies function at the molecular level, and how innovations like Lenacapavir represent not just a drug, but a new philosophy of preventive medicine. Most of all, it reinforced my belief that scientific progress is only meaningful when it reaches the people who need it most.

"More than anything, this experience reinforced my belief that scientific progress cannot be separated from questions of equity, accessibility, and human rights."
